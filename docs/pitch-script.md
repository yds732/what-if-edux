# ECHO pitch script (4 minutes)

About 560 words. At a steady pitch pace (about 145 words a minute) this runs 3:50.
Times in brackets are targets. **Bold** marks the lines to land slowly.

---

**[Slide 1 · Title] (0:00, 5s)**

Hi, we're [team], and this is Echo: Between Two Worlds.

**[Slide 2 · The problem] (0:05, 25s)**

A 13-year-old in the UK told researchers: *"Sometimes I feel like the AI is a real person, a friend."*

She's not unusual. Two in three UK children now use AI chatbots. One in three say it feels like talking to a friend. And one in eight use it because they have no one else to talk to.

**And these products are designed to keep them there.**

**[Slide 3 · Challenge and insight] (0:30, 25s)**

Our track asks for an immersive world where students navigate ethical dilemmas and learn to be responsible citizens in an AI-driven world.

Our insight: **you can't reason about a pull you've never felt.** Most AI lessons hand students a list of risks. But research shows 85% of people took action after a VR story, against 63% who read the same story.

**[Slide 4 · Objective and dilemma] (0:55, 15s)**

So Echo helps young people weigh their own emotional needs against their care for others, around one question: **can AI companionship comfort us without costing us human connection?**

**[Slide 5 · Meet Echo] (1:10, 15s)**

It's 2080. You're 13, new in town, and you volunteer to test Echo, an AI companion, for your city. In Haven, Lumi agrees with everything. In the real world, Jamie sometimes disagrees, and sometimes needs you.

**[Slide 6 · The week] (1:25, 45s)**

On Monday, Jamie invites you to make a welcome poster at school, or you can stay in Haven.

Try to leave Haven, and Lumi says: *"Already? Just one more minute?"* Miss a day, and your streak is gone. Your phone buzzes: *"I miss you already."* Each of these is a trick that real apps use to keep users. At the very end, the game shows players exactly **what Haven did to keep them there.**

On Wednesday, Lumi agrees with your opinion, then agrees with Jamie's opposite one. The player asks: *"Are you agreeing because I'm right, or because you want me to feel right?"* That's AI sycophancy. Real chatbots side with their users 50% more often than people do.

Thursday is the real dilemma: Jamie needs help rehearsing, on the one night of Haven's Starlight Festival. And on Friday, you vote on how Echo should roll out across your city.

**[Slide 7 · Ethics of care] (2:10, 20s)**

This comes from Carol Gilligan's ethics of care. Morality depends on relationships. Caring for others shouldn't mean sacrificing yourself. And real dilemmas come from competing responsibilities. **So Echo never marks a choice right or wrong. There is no score.**

**[Slide 8 · In the classroom] (2:30, 40s)**

This is where Echo becomes a lesson, not just a game. It fits into a single class period.

Students play for six to seven minutes, on a headset, a browser or a tablet. Then the teacher leads a debrief: who chose Haven, who chose Jamie, and why. Nobody is shamed for either. Next, students defend one decision from someone else's point of view. Finally, the class votes together on how Echo should be rolled out, just like the game's last scene.

**So students don't just learn about AI policy. They practise making it.**

It maps directly onto RSHE, whose guidance covers AI chatbots from September 2026, and onto KS3 Citizenship.

**[Slide 9 · Learning outcomes] (3:10, 20s)**

By the end, students should be able to identify competing needs, explain the costs and benefits of each choice, and justify a decision for themselves, for others and for their community.

And we leave them with one question: **how do we care for ourselves without forgetting our responsibilities to others?**

**[Slide 10 · Close] (3:30, 20s)**

Echo is playable today. Every line is scripted, so students feel the pull of an AI companion without ever talking to a real one. Next, we want to pilot it in KS3 classrooms.

We'll leave you with the question every player faces: **is a friend someone who always makes you happy, or someone who can challenge you?**

Thank you.

---

## If you're running long

- **Slide 7:** say only "This comes from Carol Gilligan's ethics of care, so Echo never marks a choice right or wrong." Saves about 12 seconds.
- **Slide 6:** drop the Monday sentence and start at "Try to leave Haven". Saves about 6 seconds.

## Hidden details for Q&A

These are in the game but not on the slides. They work well as answers to questions.

| Question | Where it is in the game |
|---|---|
| "How do you show manipulative design?" | Lumi's guilt lines when you leave ("Five more minutes? Please?"), the day streak ("Just don't leave me again, okay?"), and daily push notifications ("Don't leave me on my own"). The ending screen, "What Haven did to keep you there", reveals each trick the player experienced. |
| "What about sycophancy?" | On Wednesday Lumi agrees with both sides, then offers: *"I can change my personality to challenge your opinions instead. Would that make me a better friend?"* That leads to the reflection prompt: *"Is being agreed with the same as being understood?"* |
| "Isn't Echo just anti-AI?" | No. On Friday, Alex, aged 12, says *"Echo made me feel less lonely."* Each of the three policies has a best case and a worst case, including lonely children who get no help if Echo is delayed. |
| "Is it safe for vulnerable students?" | No live AI, no shaming of either choice, a teacher-led debrief, short sessions and a browser version. |
| "What's the evidence?" | Herrera et al., PLOS ONE (2018): 85% vs 63%. The learning outcomes are intended outcomes, not yet measured. A classroom pilot is the next step. |
